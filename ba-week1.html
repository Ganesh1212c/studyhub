👀Why Visualise Data?

02-05

Good decisions need good data

  • Good data
  • Understanding
  • Good decisions

Good decisions are based on an accurate understanding of good data.

Do you need to be Einstein to make sense of your data?

No. A well-chosen picture does the hard thinking for you. Pick the right chart and anyone can see the pattern.

Vision is our most powerful sense

70%sight
30%all the other senses

On the slide the eye gets 70% and the other senses (hearing, taste, touch and so on) share the remaining 30%. Most of what we take in comes through our eyes, so show data as something to see.

Benefits of visual representation

  • Communicate complex information concisely and powerfully
  • Create a “picture” for reasoning about and analyzing quantitative and conceptual information
  • Makes cognitive processing easier
  • Provides “content/information rich” view at a glance
  • Directs attention toward the content rather than methodology
  • Describe, explore, summarize a set of numbers
  • Convey a message about the significance of the data
In simple words

Numbers on their own are hard work. To decide well you must understand the data, and a picture is the fastest way to get there.

  • Sight is our strongest sense, so a chart is read in a blink while a table of numbers takes time.
  • Easier thinking: the brain spends less effort, so you can focus on what the data means, not on how it was worked out.
  • Everything at a glance: one picture can hold a lot of information and still be clear.
  • Three jobs: describe the numbers, explore them to find patterns, and summarise them with a clear message.

Think of it like this: directions to a friend's house. A list of GPS coordinates is accurate but useless in the car. A map shows the way at a glance.

🧪Types of Data

04

Data

Categorical

Defined categories or groups

Customer ratings, City

Numerical

Discrete

Counted items

Number of cars owned, Defects per hour

Continuous

Measured characteristics

Time, Pressure

In simple words

Before you pick a chart, ask what kind of data you have. Three questions sort almost everything:

  • Is it a label or a group? It is categorical. Examples: city, highest level of education, a rating like Poor / Average / Good. Even an age bucket such as "below 18" or "19 to 30" is categorical, because it is a group.
  • Can you count it in whole steps (0, 1, 2, 3...)? It is discrete. Examples: number of cars owned, number of siblings, defects per hour (you count defects).
  • Do you measure it, so any value in between is possible? It is continuous. Examples: time, pressure, weight, volume, density.

Quick test: counting gives discrete numbers, measuring gives continuous numbers, naming gives categories.

Think of it like this: money. The number of coins in your pocket is discrete. The exact weight of the coins is continuous. The type of coin (one rupee, two rupee) is categorical.

🔭Attributes of Visual Perception

06

Form

  • Length
  • Width
  • Orientation
  • Size
  • Shape
  • Curvature
  • Enclosure
  • Spatial grouping
  • Blur

Color

  • Hue
  • Intensity

Spatial

  • 2-D position
  • Direction of motion
In simple words

These are the visual cues your brain notices almost instantly, without effort (they are often called pre-attentive attributes). A chart works by turning numbers into one of these cues:

  • The height of a bar is length.
  • The place of a dot on a scatter plot is 2-D position.
  • A dark or light shade is colour intensity; red versus blue is hue.
  • The size of a bubble is size.

Not all cues are equal: the tutorial notes say our eyes are more sensitive to position than to area. That is why bar charts are easy to read and 3-D pies are not.

Think of it like this: road signs. They use colour, shape and size so a driver can read them in a split second.

☂️Four “Umbrella” Principles of Effective Visualization

07

1 Know purpose

Why am I making this? See card

2 Ensure integrity

No distortion of the truth. See card

3 Maximize data ink; minimize non-data ink

Only ink that shows data. See card

4 Show your data; annotate

Show the numbers and point out what matters. See card

In simple words

Four habits to check every table or chart against before you share it:

  • Know purpose: can I finish the sentence "My purpose in creating this is ..."?
  • Ensure integrity: is the picture honest, not just the numbers?
  • Maximize data ink: is there any decoration that tells the reader nothing? Remove it.
  • Show your data; annotate: can the reader see the actual values and what to notice?

Think of it like this: an umbrella. All four rules sit under one cover: a clear, honest picture that gets the message across.

🎯Principle 1: Know Your Purpose

08

Write a purpose statement

  • Have a purpose statement for every table or graph you create
  • Design the display to serve that purpose

“My purpose in creating this is ____________”

Example

“My purpose in creating this graph is to help the audience see that only a small percentage of the patient base are candidates for this specific therapeutic regimen.”

A purpose is not necessarily a message

In simple words

Your purpose is why the chart exists and what you want the reader to see. Knowing it first makes every other choice easier: which chart, which colours, what to leave out.

A purpose and a message are different. The purpose says what the chart is for ("help the audience see that few patients qualify"). The message is the specific statement the data proves ("only a small share of patients qualify").

Quick check: if you cannot finish the purpose sentence, do not make the chart yet.

Think of it like this: packing for a trip. First decide where you are going, then decide what goes in the bag.

⚖️Principle 2: Ensure Integrity

09

Ensuring integrity is not just being correct; it also means no distortion. The information must be correct and presented in a way that doesn't distort the truth.

Compare these two earnings-per-share graphs. The numbers are the same, but different scales convey different messages. The graph on the left is deceitful.
In simple words

Integrity means the picture tells the same story as the numbers. Correct numbers can still give a false impression if the chart is drawn in a sneaky way.

Look at the left chart. Its axis starts at $1.50, so the 1999 bar looks about 5 times shorter than the 2000 bar. In truth $2.20 is only about 1.3 times $1.64. The right chart starts at $0, so bar heights match the values.

  • For a bar or column chart, the length of the bar is the number, so the axis must start at zero.
  • Do not stretch or squeeze the scale to make a change look bigger or smaller.
  • If the axis does not start at zero, say so clearly, or use a chart type where that is fine (such as a line chart).

Think of it like this: a funhouse mirror. It does show you, but not as you really are.

Exam answer: the left graph is the deceitful one, because the y-axis does not start at zero.

📝Principle 4: Show Your Data; Annotate

11

Don't hide data… show it. Use annotations.

Annotation: Thursday is the best day.

In simple words

Do not make the reader squint at the axis to guess a value. Show the data by writing the numbers on or beside the marks.

Annotate means adding a few words or a highlight that point to what matters, such as "sales jumped after the offer". The reader then knows where to look and what to conclude.

Balance: label what matters, and delete labels that only add clutter.

Think of it like this: a museum. The painting is the data and the small plaque beside it is the annotation that tells you why it matters.

The example above uses made-up numbers to show the idea.

🪜Executing Your Display: A Three-Step Process

12

  1. Defining message

    • What am I trying to communicate?
    • What is the message?
    • How do I make that message clear at a glance?

  2. Choosing form

    • Should I use text, table, graph, or diagram?
    • Or a combination?

  3. Creating design

    • What design principles lead to quick cognitive processing and effective communication of the message?
In simple words

Always work in this order:

  • Message: decide what you want to say, in one sentence.
  • Form: choose how to show it: text, a table, a chart or a diagram.
  • Design: make it clean and easy to read so the message lands quickly.

Most mistakes come from skipping step 1 and jumping straight to making a pretty chart.

Think of it like this: cooking. Choose the dish (message), choose the pan (form), then plate it nicely (design).

Typo on the slide The slide prints 25-29 as “10,5060”. It is 105,060: the chart data says so and the column adds up to the 816,148 total. Shares are rounded, so they add to 100.1%.

Two messages the slide offers

  • Majority of AIDS cases are in the 25-44 age groups
  • AIDS is most prevalent in the 35-39 age group

Both are true, and either can be the message. They answer different questions. Pick by purpose.

The range message is the stronger one here. Ages 25-44 hold 603,226 of 816,148 cases, which is 73.9%, nearly 3 in 4. The single top group (35-39) holds only 22.4%, and it is barely ahead of 30-34 (182,857 vs 179,164, a gap of 3,693 cases).

In simple words

The message is the one clear sentence you want the viewer to walk away with. To find it, look at the data and ask: what stands out?

  • Find the biggest group and the smallest group.
  • Turn counts into shares (cases in a group divided by the total), because "nearly 3 in 4" is easier to grasp than "603,226".
  • Write the message as a short, true statement that the numbers can back up.

Here, adding up the four highlighted age groups gives 73.9% of all cases. That is a clear message you can then show with a chart.

🔍Step 2: Choosing the Form

15-16
Below are three ways to display the data to communicate the message from the distribution of AIDS sufferers. What is the most appropriate form?

Table

Exact numbers, but 13 rows to scan. No pattern jumps out.

Line chart

Shows the hump, but a line suggests a smooth trend over time. These are separate age groups.

Column chart

Each age group is its own column. The tall middle columns stand out at once.

The column chart (the one on the right of the slide).

The message is about distribution: how cases spread across age groups. Slide 19 pairs "frequency, distribution" with the histogram / column chart. The table makes you do the comparing in your head, and the line chart wrongly hints at change over time.

In simple words

Same data, three forms. To choose, ask: what must the reader see? Here they must see where most cases sit. Tall columns in the middle show that instantly.

  • Use a table when people need to look up exact values.
  • Use a line when something changes along a time axis.
  • Use columns when you compare separate groups or show how counts spread across them.

Think of it like this: choosing a vehicle. A bicycle, a bus and a truck all move things, but only one suits the trip.

🧾Choosing Form: Table or Chart?

17-18

One key decision: display the data as a table or as a chart.

Choose a table to…

  • Display the complete data set
  • Focus on a specific item (highlighted) in the context of the complete data set
  • Present a wide range of data that is difficult to scale using a graph
  • Explain how numbers are derived – how results were calculated

Choose a chart to…

  • Compare a slice of information
  • Show “cause of change” (vertical axis) vs. “effect” (horizontal axis)
  • Show change over time
  • Show patterns of data distribution (normal curve)

🧩Match the Message to the Chart

19

Choose the graph type for your message: “If your message stresses… then choose…”

If your message stresses…Then choose…In plain words
Components of one itemPie chartOne whole cut into parts that add up to 100%.
Components of multiple items100% column / stacked column chartSeveral wholes, each split into the same parts. The parts are stacked.
Item comparisonBar chartLine the items up side by side and compare their sizes.
Change over timeColumn / line chartTime runs left to right. Watch the value rise or fall.
Frequency, distributionHistogramHow many values fall in each range. Shows the shape of the data.
CorrelationPaired bar, scatter dotTwo measures for each item. Do they move together?
Outliers (slide 25)Box plotThe box holds the middle half of the values. Dots beyond the whiskers are outliers.
In simple words

This table is the heart of the week. Pick the chart by the message, not by what looks nice. Ask which of these you want the reader to see:

  • How is one thing split up? Pie chart.
  • How are several things each split up? Stacked column chart.
  • Which item is bigger? Bar chart.
  • How did it change over time? Line or column chart.
  • How are values spread out? Histogram.
  • Do two measures rise and fall together? Scatter plot.
  • Are there odd values far from the rest? Box plot.

Memory hook: stacked means parts piled up, so it shows components. Plain bars stand side by side, so they show comparison. Practice Q6 to Q12 test exactly this. See Q6 to Q12.

🧪Examples on Choice of Form

20-25

On each slide the title is the message and the chart is chosen to prove it.

  • “Sales of Product A exceed sales of B and C”

    Bar chart: item comparison

    Product A $49 million, Product B $35 million, Product C $27 million.

  • “There is no apparent relationship between tenure and sales”

    Scatter plot: how items correlate

    Each dot pairs a tenure with a sales figure (tenure across, sales up). The dots show no trend.

  • “Competitor D has the smallest share of industry sales”

    Pie chart: components of one item

    A 40%, B 30%, C 20%, D 10%. The shares add up to 100%.

  • “Earnings per share have decreased every year since 2000”

    Line chart: change over time

    $4.90, $4.81, $4.76, $4.22, $3.86 for 2000 to 2004.

  • “There are outliers in the data”

    Box plot: outliers

    A tight box with a few dots far above it.

In simple words

Read each title as a sentence the chart must prove. The key words give away the chart:

  • "exceed" compares items, so use a bar chart.
  • "relationship" asks whether two measures move together, so use a scatter plot.
  • "share" means part of a whole, so use a pie chart.
  • "every year since 2000" is change over time, so use a line chart.
  • "outliers" are odd values, so use a box plot.

Check the pie: 40 + 30 + 20 + 10 = 100. A pie only makes sense when the parts add up to a whole.

🎨Step 3: Creating the Design

26-27

Left on the slide: harder
  • 3-D columns drawn at an angle
  • No values on the columns
  • The shading adds ink but no information, and the depth makes it hard to line a column up with the axis
Right on the slide: easier

The flat chart with value labels (the right-hand design).

3-D effects distort how tall a column looks and make exact values hard to read. Plain columns with labels let the reader see the shape and read the numbers.

In simple words

Once the message and form are chosen, design is about making the chart quick to read. Both charts show the same data, but the 3-D one makes your brain work harder to decode it.

Rule of thumb: if a design choice does not help the reader see the message, remove it.

28

3-D effectsFlat 2-D shapesDepth changes how big a bar or slice looks.

Contrasting borders around objectsNo borders, or very light onesBorders are non-data ink.

In simple words

The slide shows a 3-D pie with a legend next to a flat pie with labels on the slices (Our Company 34, Competitor A 26, Competitor B 12, Competitor C 28). The flat, labelled pie wins on every count:

  • No depth to distort the slice sizes.
  • The reader reads each number straight off its slice, with no colour lookup.
  • Fewer lines and borders, so the data stands out.

Remember the four: no 3-D, labels instead of legends, no loud borders, add annotations.

🧹Graphs: Best Practices Lead to an Uncluttered Look

29

The checklist

  • Design the graph to support the message; consider using a talking head here, too
  • Use minimal grid lines, ideally none
  • Use thin lines, thin axes, thin bars, thin arrows to show trends
  • Display subtle but visible data point marks, only enough to show the trend
  • Use minimal tick marks to display scale, usually just min/max on the Y axis
  • Label axes; label graphic items
  • Opt for value labels wherever possible; delete some to avoid clutter
Cluttered (left)
  • Grid lines at every tick
  • A legend off to the side
  • Title only names the chart: “Cumulative unit sales, actual versus plan”
Clean (right)
  • No grid lines; only 0 and 180 on the Y axis
  • Values written on the points
  • Title states the message: “Cumulative actual unit sales through July exceeding plan by nearly 30K”
In simple words

A clean chart looks calm: thin lines, almost no grid, a few labels, and a title that says what to conclude.

  • Title: write the message, not just the topic.
  • Axis: show only the smallest and largest values.
  • Labels: write values on the data, but not on every single point if it gets crowded.

Think of it like this: a tidy desk. You find the one paper you need straight away.

  • Single screen: no scrolling through many pages.
  • At a glance: a few seconds is enough to understand it.

A misleading visual makes the reader believe something the data does not say. Sometimes it is done on purpose and often it is an accident. This tutorial shows five real examples, and each one breaks a rule from World 1.

📏Misleading Axes

02

“Over 100 Million People in U.S. Now Receiving Some Form of Federal Welfare”: it appears the welfare problem is spiralling out of control.

But note where the y-axis starts!

In simple words

The slide's chart has its y-axis starting at 94,000,000, not 0. So the first bar (about 97 million) looks tiny and the last bar (about 107 million) looks about 4 times taller. The real rise is only about 10%.

The mini charts above redraw it. The values are approximate, read off the slide's chart (in millions of people). Same numbers, very different feeling.

Spot it: check where the axis starts. If a bar chart's axis does not begin at zero, the bar lengths exaggerate the change.

Source on the slide: statisticshowto.com, "misleading graphs".

🧮Wrong Data

03

“The weights of the categories do not add up.”

A news channel's pie chart on the 2012 presidential run showed these three slices:

CandidateShare shown
Palin70%
Huckabee63%
Romney60%
Total193%
In simple words

A pie chart shows parts of one whole, so the slices must add up to 100%. Here they add up to 193%, so the pie cannot be right.

A common cause is that the answers overlap, for example people were allowed to pick more than one candidate. Each percentage is then a separate question and does not belong in one pie.

Fix: if the answers can overlap, show one bar per candidate. Bars do not need to add up to 100%.

Source on the slide: flowingdata.com, "Fox News makes the best pie chart ever".

⛽Incorrect Bar Chart

05

An arrow points downward to the Sept data point, yet the price actually went up. Only the percentage increase went down.

DatePetrol, Rs per litreChange from before
16 May 200433.71-
16 May 200940.62+20.5%
16 May 201471.41+75.8%
10 Sept 201880.73+13.1%
In simple words

Prices went up every time: 33.71, then 40.62, then 71.41, then 80.73. But the slide's chart draws a big arrow pointing down to the last bar with "13%". That arrow shows that the rise slowed (from +75.8% to +13.1%), not that the price fell. A reader sees a falling arrow and thinks the price dropped.

The slide's last bar is also drawn shorter than the 2014 bar even though 80.73 is more than 71.41, so the bar heights do not match the numbers.

Spot it: do the arrows, colours and bar heights all agree with the numbers? If a mark says one thing and the number says another, the chart is misleading. Show the values and let plain bars do the talking.

Check: (40.62 - 33.71) / 33.71 = 20.5%, (71.41 - 40.62) / 40.62 = 75.8%, (80.73 - 71.41) / 71.41 = 13.1%.

🥧Misleading 3-D Pie Chart

06-07

What the slides say

  • The slices closer to you appear larger than those further away
  • Compare Item A and Item C in both graphs
  • A and B look quite similar in the 3-D graph!

Why it happens (from the slide notes)

  • The eye is sensitive to the area a colour occupies
  • The sides of a 3-D slice use the same colour as the top, so the front slices paint extra area
  • Pulled-out (exploded) wedges are worse, and perspective exaggerates the front further
  • Our eyes are better at position, which is why bar charts are easier to read

Flat Four equal slices of 25% each. You can see that they are equal.

Tilted The same four 25% slices. The ones nearer you now look bigger.

In simple words

The two pies above hold exactly the same numbers. When the pie is tilted to look 3-D, the nearer slices look larger and the far ones look smaller. That is a trick of perspective, not a fact in the data.

That is why the slides ask you to compare Item A with Item C in both versions, and why A and B look alike in the 3-D picture even though they are different.

Rule: avoid 3-D charts. Use a flat chart, and where you can, use bars. Our eyes judge bar length (position) better than slice area.

Sources on the slides: noigroup.com and Korean Journal of Anesthesiology 70(3), 2017.

In simple words

The options use different words from the slide, so translate them first:

  • “One item proportional to totals” means components of one item (pie).
  • “Multiple items components” means components of multiple items (stacked column).
  • “Comparison between items” means item comparison (bar).
  • “Trend over time” means change over time (line).
  • “Frequency of items” means frequency, distribution (histogram).
  • “Correlation representation” means correlation (scatter).
  • “Outlier identification” means outliers (box plot).

Think of it like this: a pie is one cake cut into slices. A stacked column is several cakes, each shown as layers. A bar chart is a row of cakes lined up to see which is biggest.

🌍Q13 to Q15: Pick the Best Chart for the Message

Q13-15

Q13-Q15Table-1 gives the country-wise sales of items A, B, C, D and E. For each message, choose the best representation from the four options.

The question text says “questions 7, 8 and 9” but means these three questions. The answer options are pictures of charts, so the notes below give the method and the correct option letter from the practice page.

Q13“Country with the highest sales for each item”

  • “For each item” means one comparison per item: A, then B, then C, D and E.
  • You need the countries shown side by side within every item, so the biggest country is easy to pick out for each one. That is item comparison, the job of a bar chart.
  • Choose the option that lets you spot the top country for every item at a glance.

Answer: option D

Q14“Country with the highest sales across all items”

  • “Across all items” means add the items together for each country first.
  • Then you compare one total per country, which is again a comparison between items (countries).
  • Choose the option where the total for each country is easy to compare, and where only the winning country has to be found.

Answer: option A

Q15“Across all items, the country-wise proportion of item-A being sold” Select all that apply

  • “Proportion of item A by country” means parts of one whole: each country's share of item A. That is “components of one item”, the job of a pie chart.
  • Test every option with two questions: Does it show only item A? and Does it show each country's share of A?
  • More than one option can pass, so do not stop at the first good one.

Answer: options ABD

In simple words

For these three, read the message as a job and match it to the chart table:

  • Highest for each item: compare countries inside each item.
  • Highest across all items: add up first, then compare countries.
  • Proportion of one item: show shares of a single whole.

Tricky one (Q15): it is a select-all question and three of the four options are correct (A, B and D). Checking only two of them gives partial marks.

Think of it like this: three different questions about the same sales sheet. "Who is top in each item?" "Who is top overall?" "How is item A shared out?" Each needs a different picture.
